The Fed's September Dilemma: To Hike or Not to Hike?

The Case for Holding Steady

If I were a voting member of the Federal Open Market Committee in September 2026, I would vote to keep the federal funds rate unchanged at 3.50%-3.75%. While the recent surge in Brent crude prices toward $100 a barrel is undoubtedly concerning, I believe the risks of over-tightening in an already fragile geopolitical environment outweigh the benefits of a modest 25 basis point hike.

First, consider the nature of the inflationary pressure. Oil price spikes are typically transient and tend to have a temporary impact on inflation. The Fed's dual mandate is to promote maximum employment and price stability, and a short-term oil shock shouldn't derail the progress made on employment. Moreover, the Fed has limited tools to address supply-side shocks like an oil price increase. A rate hike would do little to increase oil supply or reduce geopolitical tensions.

Second, the U.S. economy is facing significant headwinds, and geopolitical tensions are elevated across multiple fronts at once. A rate hike could plausibly tip an already-strained economy toward recession, which would be detrimental to both employment and price stability in the long run. The Fed should be cautious rather than act preemptively.

The Hawkish Argument and Why It's Not Convincing

I understand the argument of the three dissenting FOMC members who preferred a 25 basis point hike in July. They likely believe the Fed needs to stay ahead of inflation expectations, and that a modest hike would send a strong signal to markets. However, I disagree with this approach for a few reasons.

First, the Fed has already raised rates significantly since the start of the tightening cycle; a modest hike in September would likely have only a marginal additional impact on inflation expectations. Second, the Fed's communication has been clear that it is data-dependent — if inflation data starts to show more persistent upward pressure, the Fed can always hike later. There's no need to act preemptively based on a single shock, even one as significant as an oil price surge.

Lastly, the Fed should be mindful of the potential unintended consequences of a rate hike right now. Higher interest rates could strengthen the U.S. dollar further, making it even more difficult for emerging markets to service dollar-denominated debt — a channel for financial instability that could spill back into the U.S. economy.

In conclusion, while the Fed's September decision is far from straightforward, I believe the risks of over-tightening outweigh the benefits of a modest hike. I would vote to keep rates unchanged and closely monitor the data in the coming months. If inflation starts to show more persistent upward pressure, the Fed can always act then. For now, patience is the better trade.
